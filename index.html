THE QUESTION

How do customers buy, and how do their habits differ?

We use exploratory analysis and RFM-based K-Means clustering to look for patterns in product sales, geography, time, and customer value.

4,338customers included in segmentation

01 / PROJECT

Project overview

Retail transactions are more useful when viewed as patterns rather than isolated rows.

PROBLEM STATEMENT

Understand purchasing behaviour from transaction data.

This project investigates how products perform, whether selected product quantities move together across invoices, how sales vary between countries and months, and whether customers can be grouped by their purchasing behaviour.

The aim is to turn a large collection of transactions into clear findings that can support better-informed retail decisions. Correlation is treated as association, not proof of cause.

02 / APPROACH

From raw data to useful patterns

01

Prepare

Remove exact duplicates, rows without product descriptions, and records with non-positive quantities or prices.

02

Explore

Compare product quantity and revenue, examine invoice-level correlation, and look at country and monthly patterns.

03

Segment

Calculate RFM features for customers, apply log(1+x), standardize the features, and use K-Means.

04

Interpret

Use the elbow method to choose four clusters and PCA to display the segments in two dimensions.

A note on cleaning

Negative quantities can represent returns, refunds, cancellations, or transaction corrections. This analysis retains positive-value sales; the report notes that the original dataset was preserved and cleaning was performed on a working copy.

What stood out in the data

FIGURE 01 · DATA PREPARATION

Records removed during preprocessing

The dataset was reduced from 541,909 original records to 524,878 cleaned records. The preprocessing chart belongs here. In the final version, add a brief explanation of the categories and counts shown in the original plot.

FIGURE 02(a) · PRODUCT PERFORMANCE

Which products sell the most?

This chart ranks products by the total quantity sold. It highlights the products that customers purchase most frequently in terms of units, helping identify high-volume products in the retail dataset.

FIGURE 02(b) · PRODUCT REVENUE

Which products generate the most revenue?

This chart ranks products by their total revenue contribution. Comparing revenue with quantity sold helps reveal that the most frequently purchased products are not necessarily the products generating the highest sales value.

FIGURE 03 · PRODUCT RELATIONSHIPS

Two products with a positive relationship

For JUMBO BAG RED RETROSPOT and JUMBO BAG STRAWBERRY, the reported Pearson correlation is approximately 0.684. Invoices with higher quantities of one tend to have higher quantities of the other. This is an association, not evidence that one product causes the other to be purchased.

FIGURE 04(a) · GEOGRAPHIC PATTERNS

Which countries account for the most sales?

The United Kingdom accounts for the largest number of units sold by a substantial margin. Comparing countries helps show how retail demand is distributed across different markets.

FIGURE 04(b) · PRODUCT PREFERENCES

Which products lead in international markets?

The report compares the leading products across selected countries. RABBIT NIGHT LIGHT is the leading product in several international markets. The UK is excluded from this comparison to make other countries easier to compare.

FIGURE 05 · TIME SERIES

Revenue rose towards the end of 2011

Monthly revenue increased substantially in the latter part of 2011 and reached its highest observed level in November. December's apparent decline should be treated cautiously because the dataset contains only part of that month.

FIGURE 06(a) · CLUSTER SELECTION

Choosing the number of customer segments

The elbow method is used to examine how the within-cluster sum of squares changes as the number of clusters increases. The analysis supports using four clusters for customer segmentation.

FIGURE 06(b) · CUSTOMER SEGMENTATION

Visualising the four customer segments

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) projects the standardised RFM customer data into two dimensions. The first two principal components explain approximately 93.86% of the variance, allowing the four K-Means clusters to be visualised.

TAKEAWAY

One dataset, several views of customer behaviour.

The analysis shows that product volume and revenue tell different stories, demand varies by country, monthly revenue changes over time, and customers differ in recency, frequency, and monetary contribution. RFM-based K-Means provides a practical way to summarize those differences.
